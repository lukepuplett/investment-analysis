# My Investing Thinking Framework

*Draft, September 2026. Built on my 2022 note "Fundamentals of economy", with the value-capture and price layers developed since.*

## Why this framework exists

Most write-ups start from the company's own story, which explains why it is busy but not whether shareholders will be paid. This framework starts lower down. An economy combines and exchanges a small set of fundamental resources, and a business is one way of rearranging them. If I can say which fundamental a company rearranges, whether it is currently the scarce one, and who keeps the profit, I have a thesis that doesn't rest on management's framing.

The framework has three layers, and each one answers a different question:

1. **Fundamentals:** where is the scarcity, or the bottleneck?
2. **Value capture:** if there is value, do shareholders get to keep it?
3. **Price:** how much of that is already paid for, and what is my margin of safety?

The second layer is the heart of the framework: the expensive mistakes happen when layer 1 is right and layer 2 is wrong.

## Layer 1: The fundamentals

### The six irreducible resources

My 2022 note names six fundamental constituents of the universe and of any economy:

- **Time**
- **Information**
- **Matter**
- **Energy & Radiation**
- **Land & Space** (including gravity and air)
- **Consciousness, Intelligence & Attention (CI&A)**

The acronym is **MISTER C**, or **C TIMERS**. The physical fundamentals are the fabric of the universe; CI&A is the experiential realm, the part that observes, wants and decides.

The list is complete as a set of *irreducible inputs*. Time and attention belong on it because they are scarce and cannot be manufactured. Things that feel like fundamentals are in fact derived from them:

- **Capital** is past time and energy stored as matter (plant, machines, buildings) and information (designs, code, know-how).
- **Institutions** (firms, courts, markets, regulators) are information plus shared attention and decisions.
- **Trust** is information about past decisions, held in many people's attention.

Keeping derived things off the list forces me to ask what a company's capital or brand is made of, and so what could replace it.

### Change, deciding and taming entropy

A key property of the economy is **change**, and its key activity is **deciding**. Value creation, meaning work and labour, is using CI&A to make decisions that rearrange the fundamentals, or to uncover new knowledge. This is **taming entropy**: a chip, a turbine or a trading platform is a low-entropy arrangement that took energy, information and decisions to create and needs more to maintain. So the first question for any business is: *which fundamentals does it take in, and what does it rearrange them into?*

### Money and wealth

Money is a store of value; wealth is the stuff, all the goods and services people and companies want and need. Prices express the ratio. Give every child in the bedroom economy 90 more Pokémon cards and the football costs 50 cards instead of 5, though the football hasn't changed.

Two consequences follow. A rise in nominal prices, including share prices, is not a rise in wealth. And **innovation is deflationary**: it increases the supply of stuff relative to money. That is good for society, and it is why layer 2 exists, because much of the gain from innovation reaches customers as lower prices rather than the innovator as profit.

### Finding the current bottleneck

At any moment one or two fundamentals limit what the economy can produce, and whoever controls that bottleneck has the strongest claim on the output. To find it, I ask:

- What are customers queueing for, prepaying or signing multi-year contracts to secure?
- Where are lead times long and lengthening?
- If this input doubled overnight, what would constrain next?

### How bottlenecks move

Bottlenecks don't stay put. Abundance in one fundamental shifts scarcity to another. My 2022 note laid out the main line of travel: with abundant energy and information we get mass-scale automation, which reduces the time humans need to spend, and the bottleneck moves to **matter and space**. Hence building up, underground, at sea, off-planet or into simulations, and more strategic weight for nations with large populations and landmass.

The AI build-out is a live example: the constraint has moved from compute to advanced chips, to the machines that make them, and increasingly to power, grid connections and sites. The note also says AI is not ingenious on its own because it has no world; it needs access to ours, through sensors, robots and experiments, so information can feed back. If so, demand keeps moving from pure information towards matter, energy and space.

The practical rule: **a bottleneck thesis has a shelf life**. I should estimate how long it lasts and what ends it: new supply, substitution, or demand moving to the next constraint.

## Layer 2: Value capture

Layer 2 asks who owns each rearrangement and who keeps the profit. A scarce input can pay customers, governments, workers or landowners instead of shareholders. Utilities sit on essential infrastructure, but regulators set their returns. Miners own scarce matter but take the commodity cycle's price and share it with host governments. Airlines rearrange time and space for millions, yet over long periods the profit has gone mostly to suppliers, airports, unions and passengers.

The gap between *value created* and *value captured by shareholders* is the most important thing this framework measures. Each heading below is a place where value can leak.

### Property rights and ownership

Does the company own the scarce thing, or rent it: spectrum licensed from a government, leased land, someone else's data, a platform dependent on an app store? Owned rights are worth more than rented ones, and state-granted rights can be withdrawn or repriced.

### Bargaining power along the value chain

Following Michael Porter's five forces, I look at who sits either side of the company and how concentrated they are. One dominant supplier and a few huge customers squeeze a company from both sides, however essential its product. If substitutes exist, the scarcity is weaker than it looks.

### Durable advantages

Scarcity is temporary unless something stops rivals copying the rearrangement. Hamilton Helmer's *7 Powers* lists the options, each pairing a benefit with a barrier:

- **Scale economies:** unit costs fall with volume, so the leader can underprice smaller rivals.
- **Network economies:** each user makes the product more valuable to others.
- **Counter-positioning:** a model incumbents can't copy without harming their existing business.
- **Switching costs:** changing supplier costs money, time or risk.
- **Branding:** customers pay more because of accumulated trust.
- **Cornered resource:** privileged access to a scarce input (patent, licence, person, supply deal).
- **Process power:** organisational know-how that takes years to replicate.

Buffett and Munger's **economic moat** asks the same thing more briefly: what protects the castle's profits? In my terms, a moat is information or attention that rivals cannot easily reproduce. Moat scores in company folders should name the specific power and its barrier.

### Regulation, licensing and political risk

Governments can grant a moat (a licence, patent or protected market) or take profits away (price caps, windfall taxes, export controls, bans). Strategic sectors such as energy, chips, space and finance attract the most attention. Who could change the rules, why, and how quickly?

### Capital intensity and returns on capital

Capital is stored time and energy. A business that must reinvest heavily to stand still can report profits that never reach shareholders as cash. The test is whether returns on invested capital beat its cost across a full cycle, not just at the peak.

### Commoditisation and competition

This is where "innovation is deflationary" bites. When a rearrangement becomes easy to copy, competitors enter, prices fall and the gains pass to customers: society gets richer, the sector's shareholders do not. Is the product becoming a commodity, and can the company move to the next scarce thing faster than the market catches up?

### Labour, tax, dilution and management

Even captured profit is shared before it reaches me. Scarce skilled **labour** bargains for a large slice, often via stock-based pay. The state takes **tax** and can change the rate. **Dilution** from shares issued to fund losses or acquisitions shrinks my claim. **Management** can capture value through empire-building, poor acquisitions and excessive pay. Share count deserves the same attention as revenue growth.

### Timing

Early in a build-out, suppliers of the bottleneck input (picks and shovels) capture most of the value. Later, as supply catches up, the input commoditises and value moves to whoever owns the customer relationship or the next scarcity. Where in the cycle does each holding sit, and is the market treating peak earnings as permanent?

## Layer 3: Price and margin of safety

Being right on layers 1 and 2 only pays if the price doesn't already assume it. For each holding I should write down what the current price implies about growth, margins and duration, and ask whether I believe it. Benjamin Graham's **margin of safety** is the gap between my estimate of value and the price; the more uncertain layer 2 is, the wider it must be. The mechanics (scenario models, sensitivity tables) are in the investment-analysis repository's AGENTS.md; this framework decides what questions they should answer.

## Inversion: how this goes wrong

Munger's advice is to invert: work out how to fail, then avoid it. The likely failure modes here are:

- **Right bottleneck, wrong captor:** buying the company that creates the value but doesn't keep it.
- **Stale bottleneck:** holding on after abundance has moved scarcity elsewhere, because the story was right for so long.
- **Narrative mistaken for bottleneck:** being in a hot theme is not controlling a constrained input.
- **Ignoring the rule-maker:** a court, regulator or government reallocates the value overnight.
- **Death by dilution:** the business succeeds but the per-share outcome is poor.
- **Paying for layer 1 twice:** the scarcity is already priced, so being right earns nothing.
- **Framework as rationalisation:** using the vocabulary to defend a position rather than test it.

## The checklist

Run this on any holding or watchlist name.

**Layer 1: Fundamentals**
- [ ] Which fundamentals (MISTER C) does this company take in, and which does it rearrange?
- [ ] Is that the current bottleneck? What is the evidence (queues, lead times, contracts)?
- [ ] How long does the bottleneck last, and what ends it? Where does scarcity move next?

**Layer 2: Value capture**
- [ ] Who owns the scarce input or the rearrangement: the company, a landlord, a licensor or a state?
- [ ] Who else is fighting over the profit: suppliers, customers, substitutes, labour, government?
- [ ] Which specific power (of Helmer's seven) lets this company keep the profit, and what is its barrier?
- [ ] Are returns on capital above the cost of capital through a whole cycle?
- [ ] Is the product commoditising? Is innovation here passing gains to customers?
- [ ] What happens to the share count, tax rate and management incentives?
- [ ] Where are we in the cycle: early (suppliers capture) or late (customers or the next scarcity capture)?

**Layer 3: Price**
- [ ] In one sentence, what does today's price assume?
- [ ] What is my margin of safety, and is it large enough given my uncertainty on layer 2?

**Inversion**
- [ ] What would make me wrong, and what would I see first?
- [ ] Am I testing this thesis or defending it?

## Worked examples

Qualitative sketches, not updated theses. Any figure names its source; the full work lives in each company's folder.

### NVDA (Nvidia)

*Layer 1:* rearranges information and energy into compute, currently a bottleneck for AI. *Layer 2:* captures unusually well for now through switching costs (its software ecosystem) and scale, but its largest customers are designing their own chips, and it relies on one leading foundry for supply. *Timing:* the classic early-cycle picks-and-shovels captor; the question is what happens when power and space replace compute as the constraint. (NVDA is held but has no repository folder yet, so this is the least evidenced example.)

### ASML

*Layer 1:* turns information, radiation and matter into the machines that make advanced chips, one step upstream of the compute bottleneck. *Layer 2:* the repository's April 2026 thesis describes it as the sole supplier of EUV lithography, which is a cornered resource and process power working together. The leaks are export controls, which decide who it may sell to, and a very concentrated customer base. Capture looks strong, so the risk sits mainly in layer 3 and in cyclical timing.

### ENR.DE (Siemens Energy)

*Layer 1:* a direct play on the bottleneck moving to energy and grid capacity as automation and AI scale. *Layer 2:* harder. Its customers are often utilities with regulated returns, and much demand is policy-driven, so governments shape pricing on both sides. The wind business (Gamesa) shows a real energy need still producing losses when competition and fixed-price contracts pass value to customers. The thesis depends on whether scarce turbines and grid equipment give Siemens Energy the pricing power to make its backlog more profitable than past contracts.

### RKLB (Rocket Lab)

*Layer 1:* rearranges energy and matter to open up access to space, the fundamental my 2022 note expects to become scarce. *Layer 2:* Electron's launch record points to process power, while Neutron remains unproven. The main leak is dilution. The repository's September 2026 delta analysis records a roughly $1.9B at-the-market share sale (about 29.3M shares) to fund the Iridium acquisition. Buying Iridium's spectrum and constellation is an attempt to own a cornered resource rather than sell rides to those who do; the question is whether the dilution buys a stronger capture position.

### HOOD (Robinhood)

*Layer 1:* rearranges information and customers' attention into trading and banking activity. *Layer 2:* the prediction-markets business shows how a rule-maker can decide capture. The repository's September 2026 watch note records a Ninth Circuit ruling (28 August 2026) that sports event contracts are not swaps and can fall under Nevada gaming law, which conflicts with a Third Circuit decision and makes Supreme Court review likely. That product line's value depends on which regulator wins, not on demand. More broadly, brokerage is commoditising (zero commissions are innovation passing gains to customers), so durable capture must come from deposits, subscriptions and brand.

### SPCE (Virgin Galactic)

*Layer 1:* space tourism rearranges energy, matter and space into an experience bought with wealthy customers' attention. A real want, but not a bottleneck anyone depends on. *Layer 2:* a case study in value captured by others. The repository's September 2026 delta analysis records shares outstanding rising from 33.0M to 151.6M between October 2025 and September 2026, while the company continued to burn cash. Even if the service works, the capital providers who funded the losses have claimed much of the upside ahead of existing shareholders.

### MRNA (Moderna, watchlist)

*Layer 1:* rearranges information (genetic sequences) into medicine. Its platform showed the information step can be fast. *Layer 2:* capture is the problem. Governments and a few large payers dominate pricing, patents are contested, rivals can copy the approach, and demand for its best-known product fell once the emergency passed. To hold it, I would want evidence of a product with pricing power and less concentrated buyers. (See `mrna/` for the September 2026 first pass.)

## Using this framework

The framework sits above the company-folder workflow. Each thesis should state, near the BLUF, its bottleneck and how long it lasts, its capture mechanism and leaks, and what is priced in. When a delta analysis records a change, first ask which layer it affects: a supply glut is layer 1, a new competitor layer 2, a share-price fall layer 3.
